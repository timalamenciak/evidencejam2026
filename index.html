What happened

A weekend spent untangling siloed, fragmented evidence

Evidence Jam 2026: Causapalooza! was a hackathon on synthesizing evidence for biodiversity conservation and restoration. We accepted 36 participants across 9 teams to prototype solutions to a common problem in environmental management: evidence that is siloed and fragmented.

The event kicked off Friday with a tour of UW's Nibi supercomputer, after which teams received their challenges. Every challenge used LLMs to synthesize ecological evidence and fell into one of three lanes. In Lane 1, teams designed pipelines that annotate journal articles using a structured causal knowledge graph schema. In Lane 2, they built reasoning agents that work with that knowledge graph. In Lane 3, they built websites that answer key ecological questions.

Teams received API keys for Brine, SHARCNET's pilot inference-as-a-service, and used a small model hosted on sustainable and sovereign Canadian hardware (Qwen3.8-27B) for their annotation and reasoning pipelines. Over the weekend, mentors from UW's School of Environment, Resources and Sustainability and from the World Wildlife Fund, Conservation Halton, NVIDIA, Dougan Consulting and Aicology guided the teams. Solutions were due Sunday, November 15 at 6 p.m.

All solutions will be shared openly in a forthcoming workshop report in Research Ideas & Outcomes.

About the challenge

The original brief, as teams received it.

One challenge, three lanes

Teams picked the lane that fit their skills. Each lane had its own judges and its own criteria, matched to the work it asked for.

Lane 1

Turn papers into a causal graph

Given a corpus of restoration ecology articles, extract their causal claims into CAMO. How much of the schema's richness you capture — and how you get there — is your call.

Teams got
The corpus, the schema, a few hand-annotated examples.
Rigor was measured by
Schema-valid output, checked against papers teams hadn't seen.

Lane 2

Reason over the claims

Build something that lets a restoration practitioner ask a question and get a trustworthy, evidence-grounded answer — without inventing anything the evidence doesn't say.

Teams got
A curated set of CAMO-structured causal claims, the schema.
Rigor was measured by
Accuracy on a held-out question set, honest confidence, graceful "I don't know."

Lane 3

Help a practitioner decide

Build an interface that helps someone planning a restoration make a call from this evidence: will it work, how sure are we, can it be undone, does it fit my site.

Teams got
CAMO-structured evidence, existing wireframes — use them or don't.
Rigor was measured by
A real practitioner walking through the interface with a live decision.

How judging worked

Each lane's judges scored its projects against that lane's criteria and named a best-in-lane winner and a sustainability prize. The overall first place and runner-up went to the two teams with the most total points. See the teams and awards.

What is the Causal Mosaic Schema?

The Causal Mosaic Schema (CAMO) is a data schema written in LinkML — a fill-in-the-blanks form for documenting causal claims. If a researcher says "X causes Y," CAMO gives you a way to document exactly how, and how confident the researcher is that it's causal rather than merely correlational.

It was developed together with philosophers of causality, including Dr. Phyllis Illari, who wrote the book on causality. Every team's starter repo included the schema, a worked example, a half-page primer and a ready-to-go environment.

Lane 1 extracted causal claims into CAMO. Lane 2 reasoned over CAMO-structured claims. Lane 3 helped practitioners act on them. Same schema throughout — which is what made it worth collaborating across lanes.
